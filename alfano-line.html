Chapter I · The Immigrant

A boy from the Sila mountains

Santo Luigi Alfano was born on 18 October 1883 in Serra Pedace, a small comune in the mountains above Cosenza, son of Donato Alfano and Grazia Campano. At eighteen he left everything he knew: he sailed from Naples and arrived in New York on 18 July 1902 aboard the Città di Milano — travelling with his younger brother Vincenzo, sixteen.

He put down roots in Washington, Pennsylvania, took work as a glassworker, and in 1909 married Teresa Astorino, herself from the province of Cosenza. Together they raised six children. Like so many immigrant families, the Alfanos took on the anglicized name “West” — by the family’s account it was one of Santo’s uncles who adopted it first, and the rest followed. Santo went by “Sandy West,” but the Alfano name never fully left; his son Donald carried it as a middle name his whole life.

Chapter II · The Proof

Why the citizenship still holds

Italian citizenship passes from parent to child — unless the parent had become a citizen of another country before the child was born. So everything hinged on one question: was Santo still Italian when his son Giovanni (John) was born in 1923? The records answer it cold.

⚠ The one honest caveat

A 2025 Italian law currently limits new claims to a grandparent born in Italy, and Santo is your great-grandfather — one generation beyond the present cap. That’s a legal question the courts are still working out, not a flaw in your family’s facts. Your line is documented and airtight; the door is what has to reopen.
